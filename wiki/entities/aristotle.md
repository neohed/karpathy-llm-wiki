# Aristotle

Greek philosopher (384–322 BCE), student of Plato, founder of the Lyceum. Among the most
systematically comprehensive thinkers of antiquity — logic, biology, ethics, politics,
aesthetics — but for the purposes of this wiki his central relevance is the *Poetics* and
its anatomisation of tragedy as a form that illuminates the structure of human error.

## Role in This Wiki

Aristotle enters this corpus as the analytical foundation beneath [[tragic-realism]]. Where
the Greek myths provided raw intuition about [[hubris]] and divine punishment, Aristotle
provided the conceptual vocabulary: *hamartia*, *peripeteia*, *catharsis*. He turned
intuition into a repeatable diagnostic framework.

## Core Contributions

### Hamartia

Literally "missing the mark" — the tragic flaw or error of judgement that sets catastrophe
in motion. Often mistranslated as moral failing, but closer in Aristotle's usage to a
cognitive error: the hero does not see what he cannot see, and the blindspot destroys him.
This is the classical anticipation of what [[kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow]] will later
formalise as systematic bias — the structure of overconfidence is very old.

### Hubris

In the *Poetics* and the *Nicomachean Ethics*, hubris is the disposition to treat others
as beneath one's concern — an excess that invites corrective catastrophe. Distinguished
from mere pride: hubris is relational, a crossing of limits that the social and cosmic
order will not tolerate. The [[tragic-realism]] curriculum treats this as the ancient
alarm bell for utopian overreach.

### Peripeteia and Recognition

The reversal (*peripeteia*) — when the action turns against the protagonist's intentions —
paired with *anagnorisis* (recognition, the moment the hero understands what has happened)
form the structural core of tragedy. This pairing is precisely what [[tragic-realism]]
trains the reader to spot in political and ideological thinking: the moment when the
utopian plan produces its opposite.

### Catharsis

The purgation or clarification of emotion that tragedy produces in the audience. Less
important for this wiki's purposes than hamartia and hubris, but worth noting: Aristotle
thought watching the mechanism of human error was educative rather than merely distressing.
Tragedy teaches pattern recognition.

## Relationship to the *Poetics*

The *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE) is short — roughly 20 dense pages in modern translation — and
survives only partially (the comedy section is lost). Its core claim: tragedy is the
imitation of a serious action that, through the reversal of fortune of a protagonist of
significant stature, produces catharsis in the audience. The *Poetics* is one of the three
primary texts for Module 1 of the [[tragic-realism-curriculum]].

## Connections

- [[sophocles-oedipus-rex]] — the primary text Aristotle uses to illustrate hamartia and
  peripeteia; Oedipus is the paradigm case
- [[tragic-realism]] — draws on the Aristotelian vocabulary throughout
- [[hubris]] — Aristotle provides the systematic definition behind the mythological intuition
- [[kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow]] — the modern psychology of bias as a secular
  descendant of hamartia; overconfidence is the contemporary form of missing the mark
- [[marcus-aurelius-meditations]] — Stoic practical ethics shares with Aristotle an
  insistence on seeing human limits clearly, though by a different route
- [[nietzsche]] — engages Aristotle's theory of tragedy directly; the "Birth of Tragedy"
  is partly a critique of the Aristotelian catharsis model
