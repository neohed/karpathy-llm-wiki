# Edmund Burke (1729–1797)

Irish-born British statesman, philosopher, and Whig MP. Best known for *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), written as a prophetic warning against the Revolution while it was still in its moderate early phase. Burke is the founding figure of modern conservatism and the original great skeptic of Enlightenment rationalism when it turns revolutionary.

He was not against change. He was against the delusion that a single generation, armed with abstract reason, could safely tear down the accumulated inheritance of centuries and replace it with a clean design.

## Core Ideas

### Organic Change vs. Radical Rupture

Society is not a blank slate. It is a living organism that has grown through centuries of trial, error, and adjustment. It cannot be safely dismantled and rebuilt according to a rational blueprint, any more than a tree can be uprooted, redesigned by committee, and replanted with confidence it will survive.

Change is legitimate — even necessary — but it must grow *from* existing forms rather than against them. Reform that respects the grain of inherited institutions is prudent. Reform that overrides them in the name of reason is reckless.

### Society as Intergenerational Partnership

One of Burke's most distinctive claims: society is a partnership "between the dead, the living, and those yet to be born." No single generation owns the social inheritance. The living are trustees of accumulated wisdom they did not create and cannot fully understand, holding it in stewardship for those who come after.

This framing makes wholesale constitutional redesign not merely risky but morally presumptuous — a kind of theft from the future by a generation that mistakes its own fashions for eternal truths.

### Prescription and Prejudice

Burke rehabilitates two words that Enlightenment rationalists treated as insults. **Prescription** — the authority that long possession and long custom confer — is not mere inertia; it is evidence of fitness. An institution that has survived centuries of challenge has been tested against reality in ways no abstract design has been. That survival is itself information.

**Prejudice** — settled habits of mind, received moral intuitions, unreflective loyalties — similarly embodies practical wisdom that individuals cannot reconstruct from first principles. To strip away prejudice in the name of reason is not liberation; it is impoverishment.

> "Prejudice is of ready application in the emergency; it previously engages the mind in a steady course of wisdom and virtue, and does not leave the man hesitating in the moment of decision."

### Tragic Realism

Human nature is flawed and imperfectible. Politics is the art of the possible under conditions of permanent human limitation — not the pursuit of perfection. The belief that one generation can remake human nature along with its institutions is the characteristic hubris of revolutionary movements, and it leads reliably to tyranny and terror.

Burke watched the French Revolution transform within months from high-minded liberation to the machinery of mass execution. He was not surprised. He had read the logic of the project clearly from the start. See [[concepts/tragic-realism]].

### Little Platoons

Liberty is not primarily protected by the state or by abstract rights declarations. It is protected by the dense network of **intermediate institutions** — family, local community, guild, church, voluntary association — that stand between the individual and central power.

Burke called these "little platoons": the first objects of affection, the nurseries of loyalty, and the practical schools of civic virtue. When revolutionary projects destroy intermediate institutions in the name of equality or national unity, they leave the individual naked before the state. The result is not freedom but subjection.

## Burke and the Burke–Hayek–Popper Triad

Burke provides the historical and moral foundation of the triad. His organic conservatism was not worked out as social science or philosophy of science — it was rooted in literary sensibility, historical observation, and moral intuition. What Hayek later formalised as [[concepts/spontaneous-order]] and [[concepts/knowledge-problem]], Burke felt as a kind of political wisdom: the recognition that inherited institutions encode more than their architects knew, and that those who would replace them understand less than they think.

His concept of [[concepts/epistemic-humility]] is pre-theoretical: he does not argue from epistemology to politics, he argues from historical catastrophe. The French Revolution *is* his argument.

His method of reform — gradual, respectful, growing from within existing forms — anticipates [[concepts/piecemeal-social-engineering]] without the scientific vocabulary Popper would later supply.

## Key Works

- *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) — the founding text of modern conservatism
- *A Vindication of Natural Society* (1756) — early (ironic) critique of radical rationalism
- *Speech on Conciliation with America* (1775) — practical statesmanship over abstract principle
- *Appeal from the New to the Old Whigs* (1791) — defence of his break with revolutionary sympathisers

---

## Characteristic Tensions

Burke is not a simple reactionary. He supported American independence, opposed the slave trade, and advocated for Catholic emancipation in Ireland. His conservatism is a defence of *functional* inherited arrangements, not of power for its own sake. He distinguishes sharply between reform (which he supports) and revolution (which he fears).

This creates a genuine tension: how do you know when an institution has accumulated wisdom versus merely accumulated privilege? Burke offers intuition and prudence as the answer, which critics find unsatisfying. Hayek and Popper sharpen the question considerably, but do not fully resolve it.
