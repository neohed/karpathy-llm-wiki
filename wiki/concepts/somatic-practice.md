# Somatic Practice

Somatic practice is the deliberate use of bodily sensation, movement, and physical awareness to regulate the nervous system, process stored trauma, and build a felt sense of safety. It proceeds from the premise that trauma is not primarily a cognitive or narrative event but a physiological one — the body encodes threat responses, tension patterns, and survival adaptations that cognitive processing alone cannot fully reach.

## Core Principle

The body holds what the mind cannot yet articulate. Chronic tension, hypervigilance, shallow breathing, and dissociation are not psychological metaphors but literal states of the nervous system. Somatic practice works by meeting the body in those states — through movement, sensation, breath, and touch — rather than attempting to reason around them.

This distinguishes somatic work from purely cognitive approaches such as reframing or analysis. A narrative account of trauma may be accurate and still leave the body in a state of alarm. Somatic intervention targets the alarm directly.

## Key Techniques

### Shaking and Physical Release
Voluntary, gentle shaking of the limbs or whole body after stress or exercise discharges residual adrenaline and trauma tension. Paired with a low hum or tonal vocalisation, shaking engages the vagus nerve — the primary pathway of the parasympathetic nervous system — shifting the body from sympathetic activation (fight-or-flight) toward rest and safety. The practice is brief (30 seconds to 2 minutes) and requires no equipment or privacy beyond a minimal space.

### Body Scan
A systematic, non-judgmental sweep of attention from toes to head, noticing sensation — tightness, warmth, numbness, pulsing — without attempting to alter it. The scan builds what practitioners call *interoceptive awareness*: the capacity to accurately read internal bodily states. This capacity is often degraded by chronic trauma and dissociation. Regular morning scanning rebuilds the baseline sense of inhabiting one's body.

### Tactile Anchoring
A textured or meaningful physical object — a wrist wrap, a wooden bead, a smooth stone — kept accessible and touched deliberately when dissociation, flashback, or emotional flooding arises. The specific sensory quality of the object (rough, warm, cool, yielding) draws attention back into the present body, interrupting the nervous system's pull toward a past threat. See [[concepts/tactile-anchoring]] for dedicated treatment.

### Vagal Engagement via Humming
The vagus nerve innervates the larynx and pharynx; tonal humming or low vocalisation directly stimulates it, activating the parasympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system. Paired with shaking, it accelerates the transition from activation to calm. The technique is discreet enough to use in semi-public settings (a parked car, a locker room).

### Embodied Movement and Power Posing
During intentional physical activity — weight training, stretching, deliberate walking — attention is redirected from outcome (reps completed, distance covered) to sensation (muscle engagement, warmth, heartbeat). This somatic immersion converts exercise from a performance into a form of embodied self-inquiry. Separately, an expansive posture (hands on hips, upright stance) held briefly before a feared action integrates affirmation physically rather than purely verbally, grounding the statement in a body that has adopted its posture.

## Two Modes of Application

**Somatic Immersion** operates within an ongoing activity. The practitioner does not pause the activity but shifts the quality of attention — from external outcome to internal sensation. Embodied weight training is the clearest example. The practice transforms a routine into a form of presence.

**Somatic Transition Ritual** uses movement to shift physiological state *between* activities. Shaking before a creative task, rolling the shoulders before posting online, stretching after content creation — these are brief somatic wedges inserted at points of transition that would otherwise carry forward the nervous system state of the preceding activity. The body is explicitly told, through movement, that a new context is beginning.

The distinction matters because transition rituals address one of trauma's most persistent effects: the collapse of context. A hypervigilant nervous system treats all contexts as continuous with the threat environment. Somatic transitions interrupt that continuity somatically rather than cognitively.

## Nervous System Rationale

The autonomic nervous system operates largely below conscious control, but it is not beyond influence. The sympathetic branch mobilises the body for threat response; the parasympathetic branch (mediated largely by the vagus nerve) governs rest, digestion, and social engagement. Chronic trauma biases the system toward sympathetic activation — a state of readiness that persists long after the original threat has passed.

Somatic practices work primarily by activating the parasympathetic pathway through its accessible entry points: breath, vocalisation, physical discharge, and slow movement. They do not suppress the sympathetic response but complete it — allowing the mobilised energy to discharge rather than accumulate. This is the physiological basis of the shaking protocol: animal nervous systems discharge threat activation through involuntary trembling after a predator encounter; somatic practice deliberately recreates this completion.

## Connections

**[[concepts/mindfulness]]** shares somatic practice's attentional quality — non-judgmental present-moment awareness — but mindfulness traditions do not always emphasise physical discharge and completion. Somatic practice adds to mindful attention an explicit model of the body as a site of stored activation that requires release, not merely observation.

**[[concepts/shadow-integration]]** gains a somatic dimension here: the body is not merely a metaphor for unconscious material but its literal site of storage. Shame, grief, and rage are held in muscle tension, bracing, and postural collapse. Somatic work can access material that remains out of reach for cognitive shadow inquiry. See [[concepts/shadow-integration]] and [[concepts/shame-release]].

**[[concepts/nervous-system-regulation]]** is the functional outcome that somatic practice serves. The techniques described here are implementation; regulation is the goal.

**[[concepts/agency]]** is rebuilt through somatic practice because the practices are small, repeatable, and self-administered. Each successful completion of a 2-minute ritual demonstrates to the nervous system — not merely the intellect — that the practitioner can act on their own behalf.

## Notes

The practices documented in [[sources/therapy-grok-survivor-tips]] are framed for a specific context: CPTSD recovery, isolated work-from-home environment, gym attendance, and content creation inhibited by shame and hypervigilance. The techniques are not context-specific; they are applications of general somatic principles. The specificity of the source makes the general principle concrete rather than abstract.
