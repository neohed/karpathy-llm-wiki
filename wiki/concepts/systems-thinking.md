# Systems Thinking

The practice of mapping social, historical, and political issues as **complex adaptive systems** rather than as morality plays with clear heroes and villains. Instead of asking "who is to blame?" or "which side is right?", systems thinking asks: what are the interacting variables, what feedback loops are operating, what are the trade-offs, and what does this configuration predict over time?

Applied to human affairs, it is the engineering mindset — the same disposition that diagnoses legacy code rather than demanding utopian rewrites — extended to society, culture, and history.

---

## Core commitments

**Map variables, not villains.** Any social phenomenon worth understanding involves a cluster of interacting causes. A systems thinker's first move is to list 5–10 contributing variables rather than anchoring on a single cause or agent. This resists the narrative demand for a protagonist and antagonist.

**Identify feedback loops.** Distinguish positive (amplifying) from negative (stabilising) feedback. Ask: does this dynamic accelerate itself, or does it generate corrective pressures? Systems that produce self-reinforcing loops behave very differently from those that self-correct — and most real social systems contain both in tension.

**Name the trade-offs.** Every stable configuration in a complex system persists because it is solving some problem, even if it creates others. Understanding *what problem a norm or institution solved* is prior to evaluating whether it should be changed. [[entities/joseph-henrich]]'s cultural-evolution framework makes this explicit: cultural practices that scale and persist typically encode adaptive information, even when their function is opaque.

**Ask what this predicts over time.** The test of a systems model is not elegance but predictive traction. A good map generates conditional predictions: *if* this feedback loop dominates, *then* the system should exhibit this pattern. The willingness to be wrong — to hold a prediction falsifiably — separates systems thinking from storytelling.

---

## The Henrich lens

[[entities/joseph-henrich]]'s work on cultural evolution provides a particularly rigorous form of systems thinking for social analysis. His core questions:

- What selection pressures shaped this norm in that society?
- What outcomes did it scale, and under what conditions?
- What happens when the norm is exported to a different ecological or institutional context?

This reframes social and historical phenomena as evolutionary outcomes — not designed by anyone, not necessarily just, but *explicable* through the logic of variation, selection, and transmission. It converts the question "is this norm good or bad?" into the prior question "what problem was this norm selected to solve, and does that problem still obtain?"

This approach is directly congenial to the engineer's relationship with legacy systems: the code is not evil because it is old and messy; it encodes solutions to real problems. The question is whether those problems are still present and whether the costs of the solution now exceed the costs it prevents.

---

## Contrast with narrative thinking

Narrative thinking is not defective — it is cognitively efficient and emotionally compelling. But it systematically misrepresents complex systems:

- It compresses multi-variable causation into single causes.
- It assigns intentionality where selection processes are operating.
- It privileges vivid cases over base rates.
- It resolves the discomfort of trade-offs by casting one side as obviously correct.

Systems thinking doesn't replace narrative — it audits it. The discipline is to notice when a narrative has resolved ambiguity too quickly and to deliberately restore the suppressed variables.

## Practice

**Variable mapping.** For any contested issue, list at least five interacting variables before forming a view. Note which ones you are confident about and which you are guessing.

**Feedback tracing.** Identify at least one positive and one negative feedback loop. Ask: which is currently dominant, and under what conditions might that change?

**Evolutionary question.** Ask Henrich-style: what selection environment would favour the norm or institution you are examining? What would have to be true of the society for this to have been adaptive?

**Time-extended prediction.** Commit to a conditional prediction: *if the system continues operating as mapped, what pattern should appear in 5 or 20 years?* Note what evidence would falsify the prediction.

This last step connects systems thinking to [[concepts/falsifiability]] — the Popperian requirement that a model be capable of being wrong.

## Relation to other practices

Systems thinking is one of the core skills in the [[sources/framework-anti-ideological-thinking]] alongside [[concepts/epistemic-humility]], [[concepts/steel-manning]], and [[concepts/falsifiability]]. It is particularly powerful when combined with epistemic humility: the systems map is always provisional and should be updated as new variables or feedback mechanisms become visible.

[[entities/jordan-peterson]] approaches adjacent territory through his emphasis on the complexity and danger of ideological simplification — the insistence that clean moral maps applied to messy systems produce catastrophic results. The engineering metaphor recurs: utopian rewrites of legacy systems ("tear it all down and start fresh") historically produce cascading failures, because legacy systems contain more implicit functionality than anyone's explicit model captures.

The antidote is iterative optimisation: small interventions, close observation of feedback, correction, repeat.
