# Epistemic Humility

## Overview

The recognition that no individual, generation, or institution possesses sufficient knowledge to optimally redesign complex social systems from first principles. Identified as the unifying theme of the [[concepts/burke-hayek-popper-triad]].

## Manifestations in the Triad

- **[[entities/edmund-burke]]**: Accumulated wisdom of evolved institutions is superior to any individual's rational blueprint.
- **[[entities/friedrich-hayek]]**: The [[concepts/knowledge-problem]] — information is dispersed, local, and tacit; cannot be centrally aggregated.
- **[[entities/karl-popper]]**: [[concepts/falsification]] — we can only ever know what is wrong, not what is certainly right; applies equally to science and politics.

## Political Implication

- Rationalist hubris — the belief that one can redesign society from scratch — is the "original political sin" of the triad's framework.
- Leads to preference for [[concepts/evolved-institutions]] and [[concepts/piecemeal-reform]].

### From [[sources/framework-for-anti-ideological-thinking]] (2026-05-07)
- Framed as the **foundation** of anti-ideological thinking — a metacognitive skill independent of political allegiance.
- Practice: when you feel certain, deliberately list "reasons this could be wrong" and "what data would falsify my view?"
- Described as a corrective to the brain's demand for certainty — the psychological driver of ideological capture.
- Connects to [[entities/joseph-henrich]]'s cultural-evolution framework: psychological tools that reward nuance over certainty scaled human cooperation more effectively than dogmatic certainty.
- Research shows it reduces overconfidence and bias without prescribing *what* to believe — making it non-partisan by design.
